# 05 — Three Sample Scripts

Pace target: about 150 wpm, so 95–115 words ≈ 38–45 s. Every factual line has a source tag `[S#]`. Lines marked ⚠️ are re-verified against the primary source before production.

---

## Script 1 — "Still Here: The Greenland Shark" (D1) ★ Pilot

### Five hook variations
1. **Statement-shock:** "There's a shark in the Arctic that is probably older than the United States." ← **selected**
2. **Question:** "How do you find the age of an animal that has no bones to count?"
3. **Visual-first:** 1.5 s of silence on a silhouette in a shaft of light, then "This one was born before the steam engine."
4. **Number:** "Four hundred years. Give or take a century."
5. **Second person:** "Something swimming under the ice right now was alive before your great-great-great-great-grandparents."

### Three script approaches
- **A — Layered reveal (selected):** age claim → who → how it was measured → number → perspective.
- **B — Detective:** start with the problem (no countable bones), build to the eye-lens solution, and reveal the age last.
- **C — Time-lapse POV:** narrate centuries passing *above* the ice while the shark barely moves below. Mostly visual, fewer facts.

### Final script (Approach A) — 108 words, ~42 s

| t | Beat | Narration |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2.5 | HOOK | There's a shark in the Arctic that is probably older than the United States. [S1] |
| 2.5–10 | SETUP | It's called the Greenland shark. It lives in water close to freezing… and moves slower than you walk. [S1][S2] |
| 10–25 | STORY | For decades, no one could tell its age. Sharks don't have the bones scientists usually count. So in 2016, researchers looked into its eyes. The core of the eye lens forms before birth — and never changes. Carbon-dating it gave an answer. [S1] |
| 25–36 | PAYOFF | The largest shark they studied was around four hundred years old. Give or take a century. And it doesn't become an adult until it's about one hundred and fifty. [S1] |
| 36–42 | EXIT | So the oldest ones down there were young before the steam engine… and they're still in no hurry. |

### Two endings
- **E1 (selected, reflective):** "…and they're still in no hurry."
- **E2 (curiosity gap):** "And we still don't know how they live this long. Or how many are down there."

### Metadata
- **Title:** "This Shark Was Born Before the Steam Engine"
- **Description:** "The Greenland shark may live for centuries. In 2016, scientists dated the lenses of its eyes. Source: Nielsen et al., Science (2016). Visuals are AI-generated reconstructions."
- **CTA:** none forced. The pinned comment gives the source link. Optional: "Follow for more things that are still here."

### Sources and fact-check notes
- [S1] Nielsen, J. et al. (2016). "Eye lens radiocarbon reveals centuries of longevity in the Greenland shark." *Science* 353(6300). Largest female: 392 ± 120 years. Sexual maturity estimated at ≥156 ± 22 years.
  - The hook ("older than the US") holds even at the lower bound of the estimate (272 years → born before ~1744; the US dates from 1776).
  - "Young before the steam engine": Newcomen's engine dates from 1712. A ~400-year-old shark, born around 1620, would have been about 90 then, which is still juvenile because maturity comes at about 150. ⚠️ The script hedges with "the oldest ones".
- [S2] Watanabe, Y. et al. (2012). Swimming speed of Greenland sharks: mean ~0.34 m/s (≈1.2 km/h), slower than human walking pace (~5 km/h). ⚠️ Confirm the citation details.

## Script 2 — "Unsolved: The Wow! Signal" (D2) — 104 words, ~41 s

**Hooks**
1. "For seventy-two seconds in 1977, something answered." ← selected
2. "One astronomer saw this on a printout and wrote one word next to it."
3. "Six characters. We've been trying to read them for nearly fifty years."
4. "It has never happened again."
5. "What would you write if the sky talked back?"

**Approaches:** A) mystery → evidence → best explanation ← selected; B) told from the printout outward; C) a countdown of what it *isn't*.

| t | Beat | Narration |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2.5 | HOOK | For seventy-two seconds in 1977, something answered. |
| 2.5–9 | SETUP | A radio telescope in Ohio, called Big Ear, was listening to the sky. A volunteer astronomer, checking the printouts days later, found this. |
| 9–24 | STORY | A signal so strong he circled it and wrote one word: Wow. It sat right near the frequency hydrogen naturally emits — a channel scientists thought aliens might use. It lasted exactly as long as the telescope was pointed at that spot. |
| 24–36 | PAYOFF | Then it was gone. Astronomers have searched that patch of sky for decades. It has never repeated. |
| 36–41 | EXIT | Maybe it was a natural flare. Maybe something else. But someone, once, heard it. |

**Endings:** E1 (selected): "…someone, once, heard it." · E2: "One recent idea blames a cloud of hydrogen that briefly lit up. The debate isn't over."

**Title:** "The 72 Seconds Nobody Can Explain"

**Fact notes:** Big Ear, Ohio State University. Detected 15 Aug 1977; Jerry R. Ehman circled "6EQUJ5" and wrote "Wow!". 72 s matches the beam-transit window. Near 1420 MHz (hydrogen line). Not re-detected. ⚠️ The 2024 "hydrogen cloud brightened by a transient source" hypothesis (Méndez et al.) must be checked against the paper before E2 is used. We do **not** say "aliens".

## Script 3 — "Thought Experiment: If the Sun Vanished" (D3) — 101 words, ~40 s

**Hooks**
1. "If the Sun vanished right now, you wouldn't know for eight minutes." ← selected
2. "The Sun you see isn't the Sun. It's the Sun eight minutes ago."
3. "Earth would keep orbiting… nothing."
4. "Here's the strangest eight minutes in the solar system."
5. "What happens first if the Sun disappears? Not the dark."

**Approaches:** A) timeline (8 min → hours → days → year) ← selected; B) one person's POV; C) "what keeps working" (deep-ocean life).

| t | Beat | Narration |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2.5 | HOOK | If the Sun vanished right now, you wouldn't know for eight minutes. |
| 2.5–10 | SETUP | That's how long its light takes to reach us. And gravity travels at the same speed. So for eight minutes, Earth keeps circling… nothing. |
| 10–25 | STORY | Then the sky goes dark. The planets linger a little longer — they're lit by older sunlight — then they wink out too. Earth stops curving and drifts off in a straight line. |
| 25–35 | PAYOFF | The cold arrives slowly. The surface freezes… but deep under the ice, warmed by Earth's own heat, the oceans stay liquid. |
| 35–40 | EXIT | And somewhere down there, life that never needed the Sun keeps going. |

**Endings:** E1 (selected): deep-sea vent life continues. · E2: "So enjoy this sunlight. It's already eight minutes old."

**Title:** "If the Sun Disappeared, You'd Have 8 Minutes"

**Fact notes:** Mean light travel time ≈ 8 min 20 s. Changes in gravity propagate at c (confirmed by GW170817). Earth would continue in a straight tangential line. Planets disappear later according to their own light-travel distance. Hydrothermal-vent chemosynthetic ecosystems do not depend on sunlight. ⚠️ Avoid exact temperature timelines unless sourced. The script deliberately keeps them qualitative.
